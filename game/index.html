How the model works

You are the board of Zorggroep Cammelot, a made-up care group that runs De Brink GP practice and Cammelot Hospital in a town of 5,000 people, about seventy of whom you see in the streets. Residents fall ill, wait at the GP, are sometimes referred, and wait again for the hospital, sometimes longer than the agreed limit.

The game squeezes a few things together for play. In the Netherlands the GP practice and the hospital are separate organisations with their own contracts with the insurers, each hires its own doctors, and a board does not pick single software tools. One board over both, buying the tools itself, is made up, so that you can steer both.

You are the board

Three advisors sit at the table, each with a card along the top of the screen showing the figures they watch.

Marieke, CEO
The budget, which comes from the care group's yearly contracts with the health insurers, and the trust of the insurer and the supervisory board.
Ruud, head of care
How many people waited too long for the hospital, for a first visit and for treatment, how many of them were urgent, the months they spent past the limit, the healthy years lost while waiting, and how tired the staff are.
Yara, IT
The record link, the outside suppliers linked into the town's computers, and the odds of a ransomware attack.

Waiting too long means past the Treeknorm. It is the longest wait the Dutch care sector agreed on: 4 weeks for a first hospital visit, 7 for treatment. It is an agreed goal, not a medical limit, and the Netherlands is looking at different limits by how urgent the care is (see Sources). The game calls it the agreed limit. It counts every wait that goes past it, over all 5,000 residents, so one person can count more than once. You see about seventy of them in the streets.

Once a season, from the first summer on, the board meets. Each advisor brings one item, they often want different things, and you pick what the board does. Between meetings you hire GPs, specialists, nurses, doctor's assistants and IT staff, build practices and rooms, and buy tools: an AI scribe, a record link between the GP and the hospital and, once it is live, three AI agents (a triage agent, a scheduling agent and a care coordinator).

The game runs day by day. You can pause it, speed it up, or click any resident to see why they are waiting.

Ghost Cammelot

Next to your town runs Ghost Cammelot. It is the same town, started from the same random seed, with the same flu winters, outages and attacks on the same days. Nobody there hires anyone or buys anything. Most figures in the game have the ghost's figure beside them in grey, and overdue waiting is counted against the ghost.

Bad luck that hits both towns doesn't count against you, but chance inside the town still does. In the game's own check on 12 towns, buying the record link one day later moved a town's ten-year overdue waiting by up to 4 months, so the game calls a difference between two plans of under 10 months too close to call.

At the end of a free game you can play the same town again, with the same people falling ill on the same days. The end screen lists your runs side by side, with what you bought first in each. Open a friend's link and their run sits in that table too.

What the AI tools did in 60 towns

I played the free-play town with the full waiting room sixty times, ten years each, with a few fixed plans, and counted how often a resident's wait for the hospital went past the agreed limit, over all 5,000 residents. One person can count more than once in ten years. As detail I counted the months spent on the list past the limit, the urgent patients among those waits, and the healthy years lost while waiting. The town follows Dutch rates for illness, referrals and deaths (see how close the town is to a real one), but the tools' effects are mine. The scheduling agent adds 30% to what the hospital can treat (the national capacity-planning model plan aims for 2% more productivity). The record link cuts the referrals that arrive without the GP's notes from 15 in 100 to 3 once every GP file in it is complete, and about 60 in 100 are on the day it goes live. Read the numbers as a comparison of plans in one town, not as what would happen in a real town.

With nothing done, 12,105 waits went past the limit: 11,753 for a first hospital visit (over 4 weeks) and 352 for treatment (over 7 weeks), 457 of them urgent. Together they spent 6,667 months past the limit. An AI scribe on its own left 12,124, slightly more, because the GPs send the extra people they see on to the same hospital. In the game the record link alone brought it to 6,109 (3,738 months past the limit), because referrals then reach the hospital with the GP's notes, and the record with the scheduling agent to 1,743 (2,138 months, 60 urgent).

Healthy years lost while waiting are an estimate. Every month on the hospital list costs part of a year in full health, more for a worn hip than for a heart check-up, from my reading of an English study (Gibbs et al. 2024, see Sources), with each resident's wait tied to one of their conditions by my own rule. All waiting for the hospital counts, not only the part past the limit. With nothing done the town lost 725 healthy years in ten years (581 to 840 at the low and high rates), with the scribe 735, with the record link 440 and with the record and the scheduling agent 155 (124 to 180). The game shows it next to Ghost Cammelot's: the number moves with the rates, and in the 60 towns the order of the plans was the same at the low and high rates.

Deaths linked to waiting are an estimate too, and small at Dutch rates: 0.7 in ten years with nothing done, 0.3 with the record and the scheduling agent. The game leaves them out and keeps them only here.

The triage agent on the record gives mixed results. It left 7,230 waits past the limit, more than the record alone, and 449 healthy years lost, about the same as the record alone. On months past the limit it left 3,551 on average, fewer than the record alone, but in 41 of the 60 towns it left more months, and a few towns where it helped a lot pull the average down. These counts include the people it wrongly tells "it's nothing". They wait at home for 10 days before they go to the GP, so their clock starts 10 days before the GP's referral, as if the agent had sent them on the day it answered.

What mattered was which tool took load off the slowest line. In this town that is the hospital, so the scheduling agent helped most. Protected time for the doctors costs more than it saves here, because at Dutch rates the GPs rarely burn out. Each of the three plans here that built the record link left fewer waits past the limit than doing nothing.

With weaker effects, and other settings for the harm of waiting

I also played the same 60 towns with weaker effects. With the scheduling agent adding 10% instead of 30%, the record and the agent left 3,111 months, still fewer than the record alone (3,738); at the model plan's 2% it left 4,897, more than the record alone. Adding protected time for the doctors gave 3,847 at 10% and 6,277 at 2%. Buying everything at once left 4,480 at 10% and 5,613 at 2%, against 6,667 with nothing done. If the record only cuts missing notes to 10 in 100, the record alone left 5,907, not far from doing nothing, and the record with the triage agent 5,408. In every one of these the AI scribe on its own changed almost nothing.

The deaths linked to waiting rest on one assumption: 6% more risk of dying for every 4 weeks past the agreed limit. With 8%, the top of the range in the cancer study, the same waits give 0.9 deaths in ten years with nothing done and 0.5 with the record and the scheduling agent, against 0.7 and 0.3 at 6%. With no harm from waiting they are none, and the months of overdue waiting stay the same.

The lab tests whether an agent reads a referral right. The game shows what happens to the town when it does: an agent that reads every letter right can still make waits longer if the hospital behind it can't treat people any faster.

The four cases

Each case is one town with three stars to earn, and the targets are mine. The game's tests play each case in a set of test towns with a few fixed plans, to check that it shows what it is meant to.

Case 1 · one year

The scribe

De Brink can't keep up. One of its two GPs is back from sick leave for a day or so a week until autumn, and people wait about a week to see a GP. Ruud, your head of care, wants a third GP at De Brink. Yara from IT would rather try an AI scribe there first: it writes the notes during the consult, so the GPs have more time for patients. Marieke points at the hospital list, where many of the people treated have waited longer than the agreed 4 weeks, and asks for a third specialist. A GP costs about €140k a year, a specialist about €220k, the scribe €5k and €3k a year for each GP. You have one year.

  • People wait less than 2 days for a GP, on average over the year.
  • Over the year, at most 4 in 10 as many people wait too long for the hospital as in Ghost Cammelot, the same town where nobody changes anything. Too long means past the agreed limit: 4 weeks for a first hospital visit, 7 weeks for treatment.
  • The GP star and the hospital star both, with at least €250k left in the budget.
What the tests show

A shorter line at the GP sends more people on to the hospital. In the test towns, one extra GP clears De Brink's line and does not earn the hospital star. One extra specialist does the reverse. The scribe on its own shortens De Brink's line, but not enough for a star. A GP and a specialist together earn all three in most towns.

Case 2 · three years

The record link

De Brink can't keep up, because one of its two GPs is back from sick leave for only a day or so a week until autumn. Yara from IT wants a triage agent at De Brink. The agent reads people's history from a record link, and Cammelot doesn't have one yet. Her team builds it between their other work, about five months of it, and it can only go live with a release of the hospital system, which in Cammelot comes twice a year. Anything else you buy for the hospital waits in the same IT queue. Paying for the record puts Zorggroep Cammelot in the red until the insurers' money comes on New Year, and each month in the red costs a little of their trust. You have three years.

  • Over the three years, people spend at most 8 in 10 as many months on the hospital list past the agreed limit as in Ghost Cammelot. Too long means past the agreed limit: 4 weeks for a first hospital visit, 7 weeks for treatment.
  • Over the three years, at most 5 in 10 as many people wait too long for the hospital as in Ghost Cammelot.
  • Both of those, with the triage agent at work for at least a year and no agent mistake while the record link is down.
What the tests show

A tool that writes into the hospital record can only go live in one of the hospital system's planned change windows, which the game calls releases. Cammelot has two a year; that is my rule for the game, and real hospitals differ.

The record on its own cuts the overdue waiting in most test towns, because referrals arrive with the GP's notes, and earns one star in about 6 in 10 of them, never all three: the hospital list stays late. The triage agent sends people on faster than the hospital can treat them. When the record goes down the agent guesses, and some of its guesses send serious cases home. A fallback protocol that stands the agents down during an outage, with an extra specialist in the second year, earns all three stars in most towns.

Case 3 · two years

The software company

De Brink can't keep up, because one of its two GPs is back from sick leave for only a day or so a week until autumn. The company that makes De Brink's practice software has been hit by ransomware in two other regions. Yara from IT expects it to reach Cammelot early next year. Every tool you buy comes from another company with its own link into the computers. A scribe at the practice is set up by its own supplier in a few weeks; tools for the hospital wait in Yara's IT queue until her team has connected them. You have two years.

  • People wait less than 2 days for a GP, on average over the two years.
  • Ransomware keeps computers in Cammelot locked for less than 30 days in all.
  • Both of those, with no more people waiting too long for the hospital than in Ghost Cammelot and at least €250k left in the budget.
What the tests show

Tested offline backups keep the lock short in every test town, but De Brink's line stays long. Tools at the GP practice, such as the scribe, go live within weeks. Tools that work inside the hospital's systems wait in the hospital's IT queue for its checks and its next release. Each new tool adds a supplier with a way in, and in some towns a second attack comes in through one of them.

Case 4 · three years

The review desk

Each season suppliers bring Cammelot Hospital new tools: a document sorter, clinic letter drafts, appointment reminders, the agents De Brink has read about. Coding agents make them quick to write. Every one still waits for Yara's IT team to link it, then for the privacy officer, the security officer and the clinical lead, who have a day a week or less for this, and for the staff who test it. The company behind the hospital record sells some of the same tools as its own modules. Those need about half the IT work, cost more each year, get dearer when the contract is renewed in year 3 and stop when that company is down. Yara would also like part of her team's time for a copy of the patient data that the hospital owns itself, so that tools which only read it need less IT work later. There are no doctors to hire in this case. You have three years.

  • Over the three years, at most 8 in 10 as many people wait too long for the hospital as in Ghost Cammelot, the same town where nobody buys anything. Too long means past the agreed limit: 4 weeks for a first hospital visit, 7 weeks for treatment.
  • At least 6 new tools working for patients on the last day, and the tools that go live in year 3 take 200 days or less on average from purchase.
  • Both of those, and no problem the reviewers missed reaches a patient.
What the tests show

In the test towns the tools wait longest for the one IT officer who links them, and much less for the three reviewers. Buying a few tools with care, and having the GP pass on the patient's story when referring, does best of the plans I tried, and even that earns all three stars in only a small share of towns. Giving part of the IT officer's time to a copy of the patient data that the hospital owns itself did not pay off at this town's size, with this many tools. In my rough count it starts to pay off at about thirty small tools that only read the data.

Where the numbers come from

Every tuning number in the game carries one of three tags in the code:

Sourced
Taken from a named source, such as the Treeknorm as the NZa publishes it, or the CBS life table.
Calibrated
Worked out by me from a real figure, for example a survey band or a pay scale, then scaled to one town.
Illustrative
Picked by me for play, with no source behind it.

Of the 255 tuning numbers in the game today, 14 are sourced, 10 calibrated and 231 illustrative. Most of what makes it playable is mine: prices, how fast people get worse while they wait, the odds of an attack, the star targets. The people rest on real tables: ages from CBS for 1 January 2026, chronic conditions by age from GP records (Nivel, published by CBS), and the Treeknorm as the NZa publishes it: 4 weeks for a first visit, 7 weeks for treatment. In the game, the ? button opens a list of every number with a badge: verified (read at the source), secondary (read in someone else's summary) or assumption (mine). The Sources page lists the real figures with links.

For example, GPs in Cammelot spend 30% of their time on paperwork (calibrated): in a 2023 LHV survey of 2,296 GPs the most common answer was 21 to 30%. The AI scribe brings that to 25% (calibrated), from a randomised trial in NEJM AI in 2025 that found about 22 minutes a day less on notes. A second trial found less.

How close the town is to a real one

Cammelot has 5,000 residents. The seventy or so you see in the streets are a sample you can follow; the score counts all 5,000. The town follows Dutch rates for ages (CBS), chronic conditions (Nivel's GP records), GP visits and referrals (Nivel) and natural deaths (the CBS life table). The extra risk of dying from waiting, used only for the deaths estimate on this page, comes from Hanna et al. (BMJ 2020): each 4 weeks of delayed cancer treatment raised the risk of death by 6%. Applying that to everyone on the hospital list is my assumption. Prices, how much time the hospital has, and what the AI tools do are mine.

When nobody does anything in the full waiting room, over ten years in three test towns, 16 in 1,000 residents die each year. A Dutch town with the same ages would lose 15.6. The GPs send 386 referrals per 1,000 residents a year, against 368 at Nivel's rate for the same ages (323 for the Netherlands as a whole; this town is older). Deaths linked to waiting are rare at these rates: about 0.013 per 1,000 a year. So the game scores the Dutch measures instead: people who waited longer than the agreed limit, and months of overdue waiting, the time people spend waiting for the hospital past it. It shows healthy years lost while waiting beside them. The game's cards and end screens leave the deaths out, because in most towns they would read a few tenths of a death; the estimates on this page come from the same runs.

What the game's AI tools are

The scribe and the agents in the game are simple rules with error rates I picked for play. The game makes no calls to a language model; the lab tests a real model, on a separate synthetic task.

None of the tools works at full strength on its first day. Staff start with about 30% of the effect and reach all of it after 120 days (illustrative; in a 2026 JAMA study only about a third of AI-scribe users used it for more than half their visits, see Sources). Training days get them there 60 days sooner, at a cost in money and staff time.

The longer the triage agent is usually right, the less the GPs check it. It misses 2% of serious cases at the start, 2 points more each year nobody has protected time to check its calls, up to 10% (illustrative; studies of automation bias find that doctors often follow a confident wrong answer).

The record link carries the GP files as they are. About 60% of them are complete when it goes live, and the GPs complete 10 points more a year, 15 more at a practice whose AI scribe is fully in use (all illustrative; I found no Dutch figure). The game assumes the specialist always opens the record.

Cammelot is a game and a synthetic experiment. The town, its people and its care group are invented. It is not a forecast and not medical advice.
